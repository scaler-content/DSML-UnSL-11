C_LATE, C_EARLY = 3.0, 1.0
def order_costs(actual, promise):
    actual, promise = np.asarray(actual, float), np.asarray(promise, float)
    return C_LATE * np.maximum(actual - promise, 0) + C_EARLY * np.maximum(promise - actual, 0)

def promise_report(actual, promise):
    actual, promise = np.asarray(actual, float), np.asarray(promise, float)
    late, early = np.maximum(actual - promise, 0), np.maximum(promise - actual, 0)
    return {"on_time_%": 100 * (actual <= promise).mean(), "late_>10min_%": 100 * (late > 10).mean(),

Step 1

Error analysis: where does the money go?

An average hides everything. We slice the validation orders and measure the cost in each slice. Two numbers matter:

  • Cost per order in the slice: how bad is it there?
  • Excess cost in total = (slice cost − overall cost) × number of orders: how much money is at stake? A tiny slice with a terrible score can matter less than a large slice that is only slightly worse.
Interactive · sort the slices two ways (overall cost 8.40)

Compare the two views. They do not agree, and that is the lesson of the first knowledge check.

Biggest money

Fine-dining and casual-dining restaurants (slow, variable kitchens) carry the largest total excess cost, and peak hours also cost more than average.

Mildly worse

Rain and OldCity.

Worst per order

No slice is catastrophic. The model is consistently decent, with identifiable weak spots.

Code: the slice table
def slice_table(frame, promise, cols):
    t = frame.assign(_promise=promise)
    t["_cost"] = order_costs(t.actual_delivery_min, t._promise)
    t["_on"] = t.actual_delivery_min <= t._promise
    overall = t._cost.mean()
    rows = []
    for c in cols:
        g = t.groupby(t[c].astype(str).replace("nan", "unknown"))
        s = g.agg(orders=("_cost", "size"), on_time=("_on", "mean"), cost=("_cost", "mean")).reset_index().rename(columns={c: "value"})
        s.insert(0, "slice", c); s["excess_cost_total"] = (s.cost - overall) * s.orders
        rows.append(s)
    return pd.concat(rows).sort_values("excess_cost_total", ascending=False)

Step 2

The unsupervised toolbox as a detective kit

We now use the tools from the unsupervised classes to diagnose and to improve. Each idea is scored on the same validation set with the same metric, in a running scoreboard. No idea gets special treatment.

2a · Data quality: can an anomaly detector find the GPS glitches?

Three options exist: a statistical rule (IQR), a domain rule ("a delivery cannot be 0 km or over 40 km"), or an unsupervised detector (Isolation Forest, LOF). With the answer key open (377 true glitches in 38,043 training orders, 0.99%), we can see which one is right. Model-based detectors flag the top 1.5% by score.

Lessons. (1) Detectors are only as good as the features you give them. (2) A generic statistic can mislead on skewed data. (3) A domain rule beats everything when the failure is known. (4) Unsupervised detectors earn their keep on unknown unknowns: failures nobody wrote a rule for.

What do we do with a flagged glitch?

Dropping the rows would throw away good orders (their other features and their label are fine). Instead we mark the distance as missing, and the model, which handles missing values natively, falls back on the other information. Two fixers were compared: the domain rule, and an Isolation Forest fitted on training data only.

def restaurant_profiles(frame, model, num=NUM_V2, min_orders=30):
    t = frame.assign(res=frame.actual_delivery_min.values - model.predict(get_X(frame, num)))
    g = t.groupby("restaurant_id")
    prof = pd.DataFrame({"res_mean": g.res.mean(), "res_std": g.res.std(), "n": g.size(),
        "peak_gap": t[t.is_peak == 1].groupby("restaurant_id").res.mean() - t[t.is_peak == 0].groupby("restaurant_id").res.mean()})
    return prof[prof.n >= min_orders].dropna()

Code: the paired bootstrap
def paired_bootstrap(actual, p_a, p_b, B=500, seed=0):
    d = order_costs(actual, p_a) - order_costs(actual, p_b)          # positive = idea B is cheaper
    rs = np.random.RandomState(seed)
    boots = np.array([d[rs.randint(0, len(d), len(d))].mean() for _ in range(B)])
    return d.mean(), np.percentile(boots, [2.5, 97.5])

Step 4

The final model

We keep what earned its place: restaurant history, style probabilities, and the glitch-distance gate. Then we tune lightly (on validation) and choose tau.

One bundle for the whole recipe

The final model needs several learned pieces: the glitch gate, a first model, restaurant profiles, the GMM, and the final model. fit_bundle learns all of them from whatever training data it is handed, and predict_bundle applies them to new orders. We can refit on Jan to May later without copying code, and nothing is ever learned from data we are only supposed to score.

Code: fit_bundle and predict_bundle
def fit_bundle(train_part, params):
    tr = fix_distance_rule(train_part)
    first = make_model().fit(get_X(tr, NUM_V2), tr.actual_delivery_min.values)
    prof_ = restaurant_profiles(tr, first)
    scaler_ = StandardScaler().fit(prof_[["res_mean", "res_std", "peak_gap"]])
    gm_ = GaussianMixture(K_STYLE, n_init=5, random_state=0).fit(scaler_.transform(prof_[["res_mean", "res_std", "peak_gap"]]))
    table = pd.DataFrame(gm_.predict_proba(scaler_.transform(prof_[["res_mean", "res_std", "peak_gap"]])), index=prof_.index, columns=STYLE_COLS)
    model = make_model(**params).fit(get_X(add_styles(tr, table), NUM_STYLE), tr.actual_delivery_min.values)
    return {"table": table, "model": model, "params": params}

def predict_bundle(b, frame):
    return b["model"].predict(get_X(add_styles(fix_distance_rule(frame), b["table"]), NUM_STYLE))

Step 6

Drift: what changed, and what did it harm?

Three signals, three questions:

  1. Data drift (PSI): has the distribution of a feature changed?
  2. Unseen categories: is the model seeing values it has never met?
  3. A novelty detector (Isolation Forest fitted on Jan to May): are new orders unusual in feature space?

A drift score: PSI

PSI compares how a feature is distributed in the reference data against new data. For numeric columns, cut the reference into 10 equal-sized bins and see how the new data fills them. For categories, compare the share of each. A tiny constant (1e-4) avoids dividing by zero for empty bins.

Code: PSI
def psi(ref, new, bins=10):
    ref, new = pd.Series(ref).dropna(), pd.Series(new).dropna()
    if ref.dtype == object or ref.dtype.name in ("category", "str", "string"):
        cats = sorted(set(ref) | set(new))
        a = ref.value_counts(normalize=True).reindex(cats).fillna(0) + 1e-4
        b = new.value_counts(normalize=True).reindex(cats).fillna(0) + 1e-4
    else:
        edges = np.unique(np.quantile(ref, np.linspace(0, 1, bins + 1))); edges[0], edges[-1] = -np.inf, np.inf
        a = pd.Series(np.histogram(ref, edges)[0] / len(ref)) + 1e-4
        b = pd.Series(np.histogram(new, edges)[0] / len(new)) + 1e-4
    return float(((b - a) * np.log(b / a)).sum())

Deliverable

The decision memo

Kabir writes one page for Ishita and Dev. This is the deliverable of an end-to-end project.

To: Ishita (Operations), Dev (Product)  ·  From: Kabir (Data)  ·  Subject: Delivery-time promise model: recommendation

Recommendation

Roll out the model in stages, with the safeguards below.

What it does

For each order, it promises the time that 75% of similar orders beat. The 75% comes from our cost assumption that one late minute costs three times one early minute.

Evidence (sealed June test, never used for any decision)

  • Cost per order about 9.3 against about 18.9 for the current rule (roughly 50% lower).
  • On time about 72% against about 56%. Late by more than 10 minutes about 5% against about 18%.
  • Our pre-registered expectation was about 8.4, and we missed it by about 11%. The miss is concentrated in the new Lakeside zone (first-week on-time about 30%) and heavy-rain days.

What we tried that did not work

PCA in place of the original features, separate models per segment, and segment ids as features. Dropping glitch rows from training gave no gain either. We report these so nobody repeats them.

What helped

The right loss and metric, past-only restaurant history, a GMM-based "kitchen style" feature, and a distance sanity gate.

Risks and mitigations

  • New zones and new restaurants: conservative buffer until enough history exists.
  • Weather extremes: watch heavy-rain slices, and review the buffer for monsoon weeks.
  • Bad inputs: GPS distance gate, with logging.
  • Silent decay: rolling on-time rate per zone, with an alarm at 55% over 80 orders. Monthly retrain.

Rollout

Start with a randomised 10% of orders in two mature zones against the current rule (guardrails: conversion, cancellations, complaints). Expand zone by zone, with Lakeside last, behind the buffer.

What I need from you

A decision on the cost ratio (3:1 is an assumption, and the optimal promise moves with it), and Finance's value for one cost unit.

The story's end

Ishita reads the memo twice. She does not ask whether the model is accurate. She asks the question that matters: "If I approve this, what will I be able to see in the first week?" Kabir shows her the zone-by-zone on-time dashboard and the alarm rule. Dev adds the A/B guardrails to the plan.

The rollout starts with 10% of orders in Downtown and Midtown. Two weeks later, the on-time rate is up, the refunds are down, and the first alarm in Lakeside, with its temporary buffer, fires on schedule, two days after launch instead of two weeks.

Kabir keeps one habit from this project: write down the expectation before you open the test, and report the gap honestly. That habit, not the model, is what made the team trust the result.
